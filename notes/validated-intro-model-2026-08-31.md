# The validated-introduction model

**Corrected:** 31 Aug 2026 · Supersedes the core objection in
`notes/access-model-review-2026-08-31.md`

---

## The flow

1. We hold a verified-open role.
2. We ask qualified recruitment firms: *"do you have a candidate with these
   qualities?"*
3. If yes, the firm shares an **anonymised profile**.
4. We put the profiles in front of the hiring company.
5. The company shortlists.
6. We introduce the selected candidate's firm to the company — and **charge for
   that introduction**.

What is sold is not a job link. It is **an employer who has already seen this
firm's specific candidate and said yes.**

---

## Why this holds up where the previous framing did not

A recruiter can find a public posting in one search. They cannot manufacture a
named buyer who has already reviewed their candidate and asked to meet them.

Three properties make the fee defensible:

- **Verifiable before payment.** The recruiter is not buying a maybe. Interest
  already exists and is specific to their person.
- **Not self-sourceable.** Cold outreach gets a recruiter a conversation.
  It does not get them a shortlist decision that has already gone their way.
- **Priced against their upside, not ours.** A firm holding a candidate the
  buyer already wants is one introduction from a placement fee of $25k–45k on
  our current inventory. $5,000 against that is cheap and obviously so.

---

## The part that may be underpriced: this manufactures the fee agreement

The hardest thing a boutique recruiter does is get a new client to sign terms.
Most of the 80k demand — small, grant-funded, never used an agency — would
refuse a blind 20% agreement outright. That was the basis of my earlier
"6–8 fee-paying targets" estimate.

Under this model the order is reversed. The employer signs terms **after** they
have seen a person they want. Desire comes first, paperwork second. That is the
single easiest moment in recruiting to get a fee agreement signed, and it means
the model creates fee-paying clients rather than filtering for them.

So the addressable demand is much wider than 6–8. The qualifying test is no
longer *"does this employer already pay agencies?"* but *"would this employer
sign terms for a candidate they have decided they want?"* — which most will.

---

## Three places this still leaks

### 1. The reveal problem

You must convince the firm to pay before naming the company, while telling them
enough to know the interest is real. Get this order wrong once and the fee is
uncollectable. The sequence has to be rigid:

| Stage | What we say | What we withhold |
|---|---|---|
| Ask | Sector, seniority, comp band, location, must-haves | Company name |
| Receive | — | — |
| Present | Anonymised profile to the company | Recruiter identity |
| Shortlist | Company picks | — |
| **Sell** | *"A [sector] org in [city], [band], has shortlisted your candidate #3. Introduction fee $5,000, due on introduction."* | **Company name** |
| Introduce | Everything | — |

**The fee agreement is signed at the Sell stage, before the name is spoken.**
That is the whole mechanic. There is no version of this that survives naming
the company first.

### 2. The candidate is not exclusive to us

The firm owns the candidate relationship, and the candidate may already be in
that company's pipeline — applied directly, or via another firm. Then the
introduction is worth nothing and we have burned credibility with both sides.

Fix: before matching, ask each firm which employers they are already engaged
with. Collect it as a standing list, not per-role, so it does not reveal the
target.

### 3. Anonymisation fails on thin-pool roles

The whole premise is that we work hard-to-fill seats, which means small
candidate populations, which means a profile can identify a person. "Red teamer
who was the only external team to bypass Anthropic's cyber safeguards" is one
individual. A company that can name the person from the profile can go around
everyone.

Fix: strip employer, tenure dates and any single-instance achievement. On very
thin pools, present capability claims rather than provenance — and accept that
some profiles cannot be safely shown at all.

## Best first cycle: Trajectory Labs × RedBlue Security

Of the ten scraped roles, one pairing is far ahead of the rest for a first run.

**Demand:** `20764` AI Cyber Red Teamer, Trajectory Labs PBC. Essential
criteria: demonstrated jailbreaks of frontier models, offensive security depth,
CTF results, published CVEs, bug bounty history.

**Supply:** RedBlue Security states it represents ex-TAO members, Pwn2Own
winners, PPP and DEF CON CTF winners, and kernel-level exploit developers.

The firm's stated roster *is* the requirement. That collapses stage 2 — no
guessing whether they have the candidate. Trajectory is also under a year old,
venture-backed in posture, and hiring on a rolling basis, so no deadline
pressure.

Caveats to go in with eyes open: the role is a **contract** engagement, so the
recruiter's economics are margin-on-rate rather than a placement fee, and $5,000
is a larger bite. And this is the thinnest pool on the list, so risk 3 above is
at its worst here.

**Second cycle:** `20741` MATS Residency — $155k–$285k, up to 30 hires a year,
matched against Thayon and Impact Ops. Larger fee headroom, but the 31 Oct
structured application round means we would be working alongside their own
process rather than instead of it.

## What to build next

1. The **qualification gate** for firms — what makes a firm eligible, since
   that gate is what earns the employer's willingness to look.
2. The **ask template** for stage 1: role described precisely enough to get a
   real yes/no, with nothing identifying.
3. The **anonymisation standard** for profiles, in both directions.
4. A one-page **introduction fee agreement**, signed at the Sell stage.

Item 4 is the one that gets skipped and the one that loses the money.
